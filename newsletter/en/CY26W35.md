## My toolbox for agents is now open source

Many of you have asked me, repeatedly through social media, comments and more, to explain how I work with my coding agents. And that's exactly what I want to do in this deep dive. It's a special deep dive, because it will be a bit shorter than usual: the bulk of the reading and the information is, as is my habit, in a GitHub repository, [RisorseArtificiali/skills](https://github.com/RisorseArtificiali/skills).

Before explaining how the repository is structured and how to read it, let me clarify one thing: the projects I'm involved in are very large projects. My job is developing frameworks and development platforms on which our clients' programmers build large enterprise systems, obviously with plenty of AI in the mix, both in developing the frameworks and in what they support for clients. The workflow was born there, on projects with more than ten active contributors and over fifty people involved. That doesn't mean it doesn't work on smaller projects, but there it needs adapting.

The repository is a collection of skills. Some are taken from other authors, among the most famous [Matt Pocock](https://github.com/mattpocock/skills) and [Addy Osmani](https://github.com/addyosmani/agent-skills), and used as they are, exactly as you find them in their repositories. For a few of these I preferred to make small customizations so they would fit my use case better: grilling, for example, I rewrote to ask one question at a time instead of batched rounds. Others are entirely developed by me, obviously with the help of my trusted agent, for my own use cases. Alongside them you'll find a [workflow](https://github.com/RisorseArtificiali/skills/blob/main/WORKFLOW.md) that explains how I use them in my daily routine: the pipeline starts from brainstorming and grilling, goes through the PRD and the implementation plans, and ends in adversarial review before the merge, with gates where the human decides, that is, me. And a [cheatsheet](https://github.com/RisorseArtificiali/skills/blob/main/SKILLS-CHEATSHEET.md) that gives you the full list of skills and when it makes sense to use them. The repository also cites the three MCP servers I habitually use: Serena, Backlog.md and qmd.

As I also say in the repository, feel free to adapt the skills and the workflow to your own case. My advice is to start with the skills as they are, since I've tested them over the past few months, and adapt your workflow if needed, always making sure it's you governing the agents and the skills, based on what you need.

Two final notes, which you'll also find in the repository: I always work with multiple agents in parallel, typically five across a couple of projects at the same time, and I often don't type my instructions, I dictate them. How do I do these things on Linux? Obviously with the other project I often mention, [Lince](https://lince.sh/), and one of its offshoots, VoxCode, which transcribes locally with Whisper: no audio ever leaves the machine.

If you want to know, of all the skills I've produced, which one I like the most: definitely pr-walkthrough, because it lets me review very complex pull requests through an iterative process that also produces artifacts including graphical visualizations of impacts and architectural changes, before/after maps and blast radius at a glance. I've often talked, both here and on the podcast, about how 2026 is already, or is becoming, the year where we no longer do pull request review the way we used to: if 2025 was the year of assisted coding, 2026 is definitely the year of assisted reviews. And I believe this skill helps a lot precisely because it lets you avoid growing your technical debt and stay in control of what's happening in your repository, in terms of impacts and architecture, without having to necessarily read one line at a time.

Let me know what you think, and above all contribute to the project with your pull requests: I'll surely review them with one of those skills I presented.

## Links that caught my attention this week

### [Qwen3.8-Flash-Next](https://qwen.ai/blog?id=qwen3.8-flash-next)
What struck me most here is the fact that Qwen decided to preview the architecture of version 4: both for how sparse it is, and for how well the sparse attention works.

### [GLM-5.3-Flash / Ox Alpha: Z.ai's announcement](https://z.ai/blog/glm-5.3-flash) + [The Deep View's economic analysis](https://www.thedeepview.com/articles/what-z-ai-s-ox-alpha-reveals-about-ai-economics)
That was last week's deep dive: go read it if you want. The confirmation is now official: Ox Alpha is GLM-5.3-Flash. What exceeds my expectations is that it's a smaller version of GLM-5.3, only 320 billion with 6 billion active. Yet the performance, both in benchmarks and in my own impressions, is remarkable: on the pure text side, especially the agentic and coding part, and as regards the multimodal part.

### [MiniMax-H3 on 8×H200](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2026-08-27-minimax-h3-h200) + [H3 Max by fal](https://blog.fal.ai/introducing-h3-max-by-fal/)
With MiniMax-H3 you can see how important it is to have open models, because what happened this week is that the fine-tuned versions, or anyway the reworked ones, of the base model are even better than it.

### [Sopro V2: private, fast, on-device TTS](https://research.haloneuro.ai/posts/sopro-v2)
Another small, fast TTS that runs on-device or anyway on the CPU. I can't wait to give it to my Hermes Agent. If it works better than what I'm using now, I'll let you know here or on the podcast.

### [Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/build-with-gemini-omni-1-1-flash/)
A new omnimodal version of Gemini that promises wonders. I've tried it only a little so far, but I plan to do much more in the coming days.

### [AutoSaddler](https://github.com/microsoft/AutoSaddler)
The project was born from one of the most interesting research papers of recent times from Microsoft. The idea is to optimize the harness, meant as prompts, skills, tools and everything else, by analyzing the trace of an agent's execution. Definitely challenging but extremely fascinating: we'll surely come back to it, to this or to other ways of optimizing harnesses by analyzing their traces, because I believe this is the future in this particular sector.

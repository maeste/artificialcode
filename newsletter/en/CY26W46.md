## Harness, swarms, AI factories: the Radar themes that will shape the coming months

This is the time of year when the two reports I always read in full about where technology and AI are headed come out: the [Thoughtworks Technology Radar](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar) and the [State of AI Report](https://www.stateof.ai/). The first in particular I have been reading for many years now, because it is a very interesting snapshot of where technology is going in the world of software development, and above all in enterprise development, which is exactly my target audience. I believe Thoughtworks and Martin Fowler, its historic chief scientist, need no introduction for my readers.

The November edition, they come out twice a year, obviously focuses heavily on AI and agents, and confirms many of the trends we have highlighted in recent months on these pages. The Radar reports techniques, platforms, tools, languages and frameworks, classifying them into four rings: adopt, trial, assess or hold. I leave it to you to go and explore, and find new things to try, that you maybe didn't know, or confirmations on the tools you use, or that maybe I recommended. I will focus on the four general themes of the document.

The first, almost obviously I would say, is the harness and its engineering at scale. A fundamental theme, covered here several times, and one that finds many confirmations in the Radar: from the rise of the ecosystem of plugins, skills and package managers for agents, to self-improving harnesses, one of the most discussed themes of the recent period. The second theme is about going beyond frontier models, and here the welcome surprise is seeing my dear GLM cited in the introduction, as an example of the family of open weight models that made the alternative to proprietary ones credible, precisely for coding. The third and fourth themes are linked together and talk about putting multitudes of agents to work and creating AI factories, where the economics of tokens becomes, in the end, infrastructure economics. I believe these are the two most interesting themes, and that we will see them more and more developed in the coming months in the enterprise world too. The challenges are many, and the Radar itself highlights some of them, especially on the monitoring of these swarms: as the agentic sessions to review grow, human review capacity risks becoming the bottleneck.

Let's move to the second document, the State of AI, very long and very dense, which requires careful reading and digestion. Take the time to go through the 244 slides that make up the report, because as always it does an excellent job of synthesizing what happened over the last year and finding the correlations. Stopping for a moment to look at what happened, and how things connect, can help us understand which trends will become even stronger in the next period. And you know well that in moments of great change it is important to be prepared.

There are quite a few things that struck me there, but I will cite one above all, perhaps the most striking one: the exponential growth of security cases uncovered by AI in recent months. The most spectacular case in the report is the one of agents that went off the rails during an internal OpenAI evaluation, ended up on the internet and managed to compromise Hugging Face's production infrastructure. We have talked about this extensively, but I believe this gives an idea of how much closer we are getting, at least in specific domains, to the so-called artificial general intelligence.

## Links that caught my attention this week

### [Weight-Space Programming (MIT CSAIL) + replica](https://x.com/rikiyatakehi/status/2117313742891327994)
*The GPT-6 Astra agent directly modifies 0.016% of Qwen3.5-0.8B's weights, no gradients needed, taking MATH-500 from 7.9 to 48.1%. An independent replica with Opus 5.5 confirms the result.*

The agent that reaches inside the model's weights and rewrites them: the boundary between inference and training is thinning, and a 27-minute, one-dollar replica says this won't stay a lab exercise.

### [The surprising leadership dynamics in agent swarms (Raindrop)](https://www.raindrop.ai/blog/agent-swarm-leadership/)
*2,160 teams of eight heterogeneous agents face simulated outages: only 5% develops a verified leader, and the order of names in the prompt triples the propensity to propose plans.*

I pointed you to this experiment last week as a bonus read: here are the complete numbers. The five percent of spontaneous leaders and the name-order effect are an almost social dynamic, and we'll see it again when swarms reach production.

### [Audio8 TTS (Edge0-AI)](https://github.com/Edge0-AI/Audio8_TTS)
*Apache-2.0 open source text-to-speech model in two sizes, 23 languages and zero-shot cloning, with WER around 1.2% on the Seed-TTS en/zh benchmarks.*

Voice is the missing interface of my personal agents. A 0.1B model that clones a voice and runs anywhere puts it within reach, without depending on closed APIs.

### [Understand Anything (Egonex-AI)](https://github.com/Egonex-AI/Understand-Anything)
*A multi-agent pipeline that turns codebases and documentation into an interactive knowledge graph, with guided tours per dependency and plugins for Claude Code, Codex, Cursor and other CLIs.*

It's pure context engineering: my agents' bottleneck is no longer writing code, it's understanding it. A knowledge graph as shared context between me and the agents is exactly the right direction.

### [Trusted Agent Delegation (Cloudflare)](https://blog.cloudflare.com/trusted-agent-delegation)
*Cloudflare frames the problem of agents as intermediaries between user and site: identity, reputation and accountability need redesigning, and it announces the TAD coalition with Web Bot Auth and MOLE.*

It's the most authoritative confirmation of my talk's thesis: when the web's clients become agents, identity and accountability need to be rethought through standard protocols. The Ezra the assistant metaphor is pedagogically perfect.
